Why Cities Are Becoming Refuges for Bees

Urban beekeeping has expanded rapidly over the past decade, and researchers are
increasingly finding that metropolitan areas can sustain healthier pollinator
populations than the agricultural landscapes surrounding them. The explanation is
counterintuitive but straightforward: intensive monoculture farming produces vast
expanses that bloom simultaneously and then offer nothing for the remainder of
the season, whereas a city's gardens, parks, balconies and neglected verges
provide a staggered succession of flowering plants from early spring until late
autumn.

Pesticide exposure compounds the disparity. Commercial fields are treated
repeatedly with neonicotinoids and other systemic compounds that persist in
pollen and nectar, while municipal green spaces are, by comparison, sprayed
sparingly. Ecologists studying hives in Berlin, London and Melbourne have
documented consistently lower contamination in urban colonies.

There are limits to the optimism, however. Municipal authorities warn that the
enthusiasm for rooftop hives can be self-defeating. Honeybees are a managed,
essentially agricultural species, and concentrating them in dense neighbourhoods
places them in direct competition with wild bees, many of which are solitary,
specialised and far more vulnerable to displacement. The conservation priority,
entomologists argue, ought to be habitat rather than hives: planting native
species, leaving deadwood undisturbed, and resisting the impulse to mow.
